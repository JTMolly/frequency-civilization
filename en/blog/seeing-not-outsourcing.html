Seeing, Then Not Outsourcing the Work

Anxiety can stay; the steering wheel stays in your own hands | 2026.11

1. Seeing

In plain words

Seeing, and pushing a feeling down, are two different things.

When anxiety shows up, we don't have to pretend it isn't there, and we don't have to scold ourselves for being anxious again. It's simply a reaction people have.

We only need to do one more thing: know that it's there, listen to what it's trying to say, and then let the present self decide whether or not to act on it.

In our worldview, this step is called the steering layer.

The idea is simple: every voice inside is allowed to speak. Worry, fear, intuition, hope: we're willing to hear all of them.

But the steering wheel stays in the hands of the present you, and once you decide, the present you carries the consequences too.

An everyday example

Lying awake at midnight, your mind keeps running tomorrow's meeting.

There used to be two options: force yourself not to think about it (and sleep even less), or just keep thinking (until three in the morning).

Seeing is a third option: "I'm afraid I'll speak badly tomorrow." Just that one sentence.

Nothing has changed, but you slowly shift from "someone pushed around by anxiety" to "someone who knows they are anxious."

Those two people may well make different decisions the next morning.

Research reference | Accepting and labeling emotions

Sixty people with anxiety or mood difficulties watched an emotionally provoking film. The group told beforehand to "accept" their emotions showed a drop in heart rate afterward and recovered from negative feelings faster; the group told to "suppress" them showed a rise in heart rate. During the film itself, both groups reported similar distress; the difference appeared in the recovery afterward. (Campbell-Sills, Barlow, Brown & Hofmann, Behaviour Research and Therapy, 2006)

Giving an emotion a name does something on its own. In a brain-imaging study, when people labeled a negative emotion they saw with a single word (for example, "angry"), activity in the amygdala, which is linked to emotional reactions, went down, while activity in a prefrontal region involved in regulation went up. (Lieberman et al., Psychological Science, 2007)

Pooling 151 studies, people higher in "psychological inflexibility" (being led around by thoughts and feelings, and giving up what matters in order to avoid them) had poorer well-being, with a correlation of about −0.47. This is cross-sectional data: it can only say the two are related, not which causes which. (Ong, Barthel & Hofmann, Behavior Therapy, 2024)

One thing we want to make clear: these studies are about the practice of "accepting and labeling emotions." They do not prove the idea of a steering layer.

The steering layer is the framework we use to understand this; these studies are one of the reasons we chose this practice.

  • Campbell-Sills L., Barlow D.H., Brown T.A., Hofmann S.G. (2006). Effects of suppression and acceptance on emotional responses of individuals with anxiety and mood disorders. Behaviour Research and Therapy, 44, 1251–1263. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16300723/
  • Lieberman M.D. et al. (2007). Putting feelings into words: Affect labeling disrupts amygdala activity in response to affective stimuli. Psychological Science, 18(5), 421–428. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17576282/
  • Ong C.W., Barthel A.L., Hofmann S.G. (2024). The relationship between psychological inflexibility and well-being in adults: A meta-analysis of the Acceptance and Action Questionnaire. Behavior Therapy, 55(1), 26–41. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38216235/

2. Face what needs facing

In plain words

This passage has two layers, and we'll take them slowly, one at a time.

The first layer is a belief: some situations we don't get to choose. But how we respond shapes what the experience finally becomes.

This is our view, not a research conclusion.

We also don't use this sentence to explain other people's pain. No one has the right to tell someone who is suffering, "This is what you were meant to go through."

The second layer is a practice, and one you can check later.

The things we keep putting off and don't dare to face don't slowly shrink just because we keep putting them off.

They may come back in a different form, and often again and again.

An everyday example

Think of that phone call you've put off for three weeks, the one you don't dare to make.

Over those three weeks, you think of it once a day, and each time your chest tightens. Add it up: it has tightened twenty-some times already.

When you finally make the call, it might feel bad for ten minutes.

That is the difference between "hurting that one time" and "hurting many times."

Research reference | Procrastination and stress

A line of research understands "procrastination" as a form of avoidance: we push away things that would feel uncomfortable to face. Taken together, habitual procrastinators report more stress, and what mainly explains the link is the path of "coping with emotions by avoiding them." (Sirois & Kitner, European Journal of Personality, 2015)

A 2025 multi-sample meta-analysis found that chronic procrastination is associated with poorer self-rated health, and that part of this can be explained by higher stress. (Sirois & Monaghan, British Journal of Health Psychology, 2025)

These two papers are about procrastination and stress, and they support the practice of "facing what needs facing."

As for "the big turning points can't be changed," that is our belief. Research has not proven it, and will not.

  • Sirois F.M., Kitner R. (2015). Less adaptive or more maladaptive? A meta-analytic investigation of procrastination and coping. European Journal of Personality. https://doi.org/10.1002/per.1985
  • Sirois F.M., Monaghan C. (2025). Procrastination, stress, and self-rated health: A multi-sample meta-analysis. British Journal of Health Psychology. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42569817/

3. Don't outsource the work

In plain words

Between admiring someone and handing yourself over to someone, there is a line.

On this side of the line: they can do it, good, I can watch and learn.

On the other side: they'll decide for me, they know how I should live, I can't manage without them.

Cross that line, and it becomes attachment.

What needs the most care about attachment is that each time, you push the task of "judging for yourself" a little further outward.

Push it long enough, and the you who used to judge for yourself gets fewer and fewer chances to practice.

This line doesn't only exist between teachers and students.

With a partner, a boss, a very persuasive friend, even with AI, it is really the same.

An everyday example

Whenever something happens, your first reaction is to message that person: "What should I do?"

If they reply, you do as they say; if they don't, you feel uneasy all day.

Until one day they get it wrong, and you follow anyway.

Only then do you notice that you never even paused to ask, "Does this sound right?"

That moment may be worth a look back: which piece of my own work have I been handing out for too long?

Research reference | Judgment under anxiety, and a sense of control

Eight experiments found that when people are anxious, they have less confidence in themselves, so they seek advice more and follow it more. More importantly, anxious people fail to tell good advice from bad, and they keep listening even when the advisor has an obvious conflict of interest. (Gino, Brooks & Schweitzer, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2012)

"Helplessness" has been studied for fifty years. The original theory was that "helplessness is learned." In 2016 the two original authors revisited the neuroscience evidence and revised their account: when faced with something we can't control, passivity is the default response; what is actually learned is the experience that "I can influence the outcome." This conclusion comes mainly from animal research, and whether it transfers directly to humans is still being debated. (Maier & Seligman, Psychological Review, 2016)

These two papers say: when people are anxious, they more easily hand their judgment to others, and the sense that "I can influence the outcome" can be built up slowly.

They support the practice of "doing your own work," but they do not prove what "power" ultimately is.

  • Gino F., Brooks A.W., Schweitzer M.E. (2012). Anxiety, advice, and the ability to discern: Feeling anxious motivates individuals to seek and use advice. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 102(3), 497–512. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22121890/
  • Maier S.F., Seligman M.E.P. (2016). Learned helplessness at fifty: Insights from neuroscience. Psychological Review, 123(4), 349–367. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4920136/

4. What we believe

These are our observations and beliefs. They are not research conclusions.

  1. Anxiety and fear are part of being human; there's no rush to get rid of them. Seeing them, and practicing not being led around by them, is enough.
  2. Every voice inside deserves to be heard properly. But the decision still rests with the present self, and once a decision is made, we're willing to carry its consequences.
  3. Some situations we can't choose, but we can still choose how to respond. We can't change the big turning points, but we can decide how we walk through them.
  4. Growth doesn't have to wait for a breakdown. "I choose to no longer grow through pain. I choose to complete my evolution with love, wisdom, and steadiness." (Molly, personal notes, 2025-04, translated from Chinese)
  5. Keep the power of judgment in your own hands. Good teachers, courses and tools make you need them less the more you use them. "Real frequency education is what makes you clearer, freer, and less and less in need of me." (Molly, post, 2025-09, translated from Chinese)
  6. "The world doesn't need heroes. It needs more people who are awake and willing to take responsibility for themselves." (Molly, spoken, 2026-11-13, translated from Chinese)

5. Three questions

If you've read this far, pause for a moment and keep these three sentences. The next time your chest tightens, take them out and ask yourself, slowly:

  • What am I afraid of right now?
  • Which part of this is my own work?
  • Who am I handing my power to?
  • What am I afraid of right now?
  • Which part of this is my own work?
  • Who am I handing my power to?
