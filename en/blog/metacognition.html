Waking up ahead of time: what kind of ability does it take?

On metacognition, and the practice of regular blank space | 2026.11

In plain words

Metacognition means knowing how you are thinking.

Usually, when we think about something, we just follow our thoughts wherever they go. Metacognition adds one step: take half a step back and notice which way of thinking you are using right now to make sense of what is in front of you.

An everyday example

You are in a meeting. A colleague says something that gets under your skin, and you are about to snap back.

Right then, a thought pops up: "Right now I really want to win."

That moment is metacognition.

Nothing about the situation has changed, and neither has your colleague. But now you have a choice: carry on with your usual reaction, or respond in a different way.

Why we also need "regular blank space"

Seeing your own thoughts and having time to adjust them are two different things.

Many people can see their thoughts just fine. They are simply rushing through every day, with no quiet stretch of time to think over what they just noticed.

Sometimes a major event forces a person to stop and rethink the way of living they were used to.

Regular blank space means we clear that time ourselves first, so we do not have to wait for something to happen before we start looking back.

What practice gives you

In plain words: When we can see how we are thinking, we have a better chance of making our own choices, and we follow habit or other people's words a little less. This is what we call "rewriting the script."

Research reference | Understanding yourself, and well-being

Some research separated "thinking about yourself often" from "understanding yourself," and found that the part linked to life satisfaction and psychological well-being was "understanding yourself" (121 university students, 2011). Another study of 227 people (2014) found something similar: simply reflecting often is not necessarily better, and whether you can see yourself clearly is the part linked to well-being.

As for "being less easily swayed by other people's opinions," the research results so far are mixed, and some small experiments even found the opposite. So we keep this part as Molly's own experience and share it as it is.

  • Harrington, R., & Loffredo, D. A. (2011). Insight, rumination, and self-reflection as predictors of well-being. The Journal of Psychology, 145(1), 39–57. https://doi.org/10.1080/00223980.2010.528072
  • Stein, D., & Grant, A. M. (2014). Disentangling the relationships among self-reflection, insight, and subjective well-being. The Journal of Psychology, 148(5), 505–522. https://doi.org/10.1080/00223980.2013.810128

After you have practiced for a while, there are more benefits

For every point below, we have marked whether it is "backed by research" or "personal experience." The personal experience comes from what Molly has built up over many years of practice, and no research has directly tested it yet.

1. When you can see the root, you are less likely to keep starting over

Personal experience.

In plain words: When the same thing keeps happening, you can try asking yourself one more question, "Why am I doing this again?" and you have a better chance of finding the reason behind it.

2. Working on the root, beyond the surface

Backed by research (this covers the "avoidance" half).

Research reference | Avoidance, and later stressful events

One study followed 1,211 people for ten years. It found that the more a person habitually dealt with stress by avoiding it at the start, the more stressful events that person ran into four years later.

One thing to make clear: this study looked at people in their fifties and sixties, and it measured "a habit of avoidance," not metacognition. As for "people who can see the essence are better at solving it," that is Molly's own experience.

  • Holahan, C. J., Moos, R. H., Holahan, C. K., Brennan, P. L., & Schutte, K. K. (2005). Stress generation, avoidance coping, and depressive symptoms: A 10-year model. Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 73(4), 658–666. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.73.4.658

3. Less easily carried away by manipulative talk

Related research (indirect).

Research reference | Learning about manipulation tactics in advance

A set of studies in 2022 ran seven experiments. Participants first learned about some common manipulation techniques, such as using emotional language, offering only two options, or blaming a scapegoat.

Six of them were randomized experiments with 6,464 people in total, and there was also a field test on YouTube with 22,632 participants. The results showed that people who had learned about these techniques beforehand found it easier to recognize them, and were better at telling which content was trustworthy.

That said, what these studies trained was "recognizing other people's techniques," which is somewhat different from "understanding yourself," so we have marked it as indirectly related. As for "unconsciously living the life others think is good," that is Molly's own experience.

  • Roozenbeek, J., van der Linden, S., Goldberg, B., Rathje, S., & Lewandowsky, S. (2022). Psychological inoculation improves resilience against misinformation on social media. Science Advances, 8(34), eabo6254. https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abo6254

4. The better you read yourself, the better you read others

The first half is backed by research, and the second half is personal experience.

Research reference | Getting to know the different parts inside yourself

A 2017 study had two groups of people (80 and 81 people) spend three months practicing how to recognize "the different parts inside themselves."

It found that the more parts a person could recognize, the more that person improved at understanding what other people think and intend.

This study measured "understanding what others are thinking," and it did not measure empathy. So for the part about "empathy will be higher," we have marked it as personal experience.

  • Böckler, A., Herrmann, L., Trautwein, F.-M., Holmes, T., & Singer, T. (2017). Know thy selves: Learning to understand oneself increases the ability to understand others. Journal of Cognitive Enhancement, 1(2), 197–209. https://doi.org/10.1007/s41465-017-0023-6

Personal experience.

Our understanding is this: as we slowly come to see the reasons behind things, we are less easily led around by our emotions. Of course, emotions are also worth listening to, because they carry messages themselves.

5. The better you know yourself, the more at ease you are alone

Personal experience.

In plain words: Feeling empty when you are alone is sometimes because you do not yet know yourself very well. As you slowly get to know yourself, you also come to know more and more how to keep yourself company when you are alone.

6. The more you accept yourself, the better you respect others

Personal experience.

In plain words: As we slowly understand why we are the way we are, we are less quick to blame ourselves. When we look at other people, we can also be a bit more understanding.

The cost of overdoing it

In plain words: Living your days while also watching how you live them is like opening two viewpoints at once. You see more, and it also takes more energy.

Research reference | Putting your attention on yourself

First, there are actually two different ways to put your attention on yourself. A 1999 study divided them into "reflection" and "rumination": reflection means observing yourself with curiosity, and rumination means going over the same thing again and again without being able to stop. A person can come to understand herself better and, at the same time, feel worse. The researchers called this the "paradox of self-absorption."

Second, a 2002 meta-analysis that combined 226 effect sizes found that, overall, the more often people put their attention on themselves, the more negative emotion they had. Within that, the link between rumination and negative emotion was stronger than the link for self-focus in general.

Third, a study published in Science in 2010 used phones to ask people at random "What are you thinking about right now?" and found that we often think about things other than what is happening in front of us, and that these are usually also the times we are less happy.

Fourth, a 2001 golf-putting experiment found that for a movement someone has already mastered, watching yourself step by step under pressure can make performance worse.

  • Trapnell, P. D., & Campbell, J. D. (1999). Private self-consciousness and the five-factor model of personality: Distinguishing rumination from reflection. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 76(2), 284–304. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.76.2.284
  • Mor, N., & Winquist, J. (2002). Self-focused attention and negative affect: A meta-analysis. Psychological Bulletin, 128(4), 638–662. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.128.4.638
  • Killingsworth, M. A., & Gilbert, D. T. (2010). A wandering mind is an unhappy mind. Science, 330(6006), 932. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1192439
  • Beilock, S. L., & Carr, T. H. (2001). On the fragility of skilled performance: What governs choking under pressure? Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 130(4), 701–725.

So blank space has to include "really zoning out"

In plain words: Blank space can take two forms. One is sitting down and looking back at yourself, and that is practice. The other is setting aside observation and analysis for a while so you can really rest. Both are worth making room for in your life.

Research reference | Rest is not idleness

A 2012 review summarized how the brain works at rest, and its title is "Rest Is Not Idleness." When people zone out, the brain still handles memory, imagines the future, and sorts out feelings about relationships. The authors believe we need to find a balance between focusing outward and resting inward.

  • Immordino-Yang, M. H., Christodoulou, J. A., & Singh, V. (2012). Rest is not idleness: Implications of the brain's default mode for human development and education. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 7(4), 352–364. https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691612447308

One thing to make clear: most of the studies above come from questionnaires and lab tasks, and they looked at self-focus, rumination and rest in ordinary people. They did not directly study "people who have practiced metacognition until they are very good at it." The benefits and costs of practice come from Molly's own first-hand observation of herself over a year or more of practice.

How it relates to the steering layer

In our worldview, the steering layer means this: listen to every voice, with the steering wheel in the hands of the you of this moment.

To hear all those voices, though, we first need to learn to step back half a step and notice what we are thinking and feeling.

So metacognition is like the first step through the door.

Once we are inside, we still make our own decisions and take responsibility for our own choices.

What the research says, and what it doesn't

Research reference | Studies on metacognition and the brain

Metacognition can be practiced.

The Education Endowment Foundation (EEF) in the UK reviewed 355 studies and found that metacognitive methods can be learned through teaching. Students who used these methods made, on average, 8 extra months of learning progress in a year (updated May 2025).

Another meta-analysis, from 2018, pooled 48 teaching studies. It found that the teaching helped right away, and the effect was still there when students were tested again after some time (effect size 0.50, and 0.63 at follow-up).

So can long-term practice change the brain as well?

A 2011 study followed London taxi drivers through four years of training. It found that among the drivers who passed the licensing exam, gray matter increased in the posterior hippocampus, the part of the brain that handles spatial memory. The drivers who did not pass, and the control group, did not show this change.

That said, there are two things we want to be clear about.

First, the studies above looked at students' learning and at skills such as finding your way around. We cannot use these results to conclude that every self-awareness practice would have the same effect.

Second, whether awareness practice changes brain structure is still unsettled. A small 2011 study (16 people versus 17 people) observed changes, but a 2022 randomized study with 218 participants did not see the same result.

As for the idea of "waking up ahead of time" that we mentioned, and the idea that "metacognition is the precondition for the steering layer," no study has directly tested either one yet.

  • Education Endowment Foundation, Teaching and Learning Toolkit: Metacognition and self-regulation (updated May 2025) https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/teaching-learning-toolkit/metacognition-and-self-regulation
  • de Boer, H., Donker, A. S., Kostons, D. D. N. M., & van der Werf, G. P. C. (2018). Long-term effects of metacognitive strategy instruction on student academic performance: A meta-analysis. Educational Research Review, 24, 98–115. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2018.03.002
  • Woollett, K., & Maguire, E. A. (2011). Acquiring "the Knowledge" of London's layout drives structural brain changes. Current Biology, 21(24), 2109–2114. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2011.11.018
  • Hölzel, B. K., et al. (2011). Mindfulness practice leads to increases in regional brain gray matter density. Psychiatry Research: Neuroimaging, 191(1), 36–43. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pscychresns.2010.08.006
  • Kral, T. R. A., et al. (2022). Absence of structural brain changes from mindfulness-based stress reduction: Two combined randomized controlled trials. Science Advances, 8(20), eabk3316. https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abk3316
