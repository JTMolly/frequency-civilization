When we admire, when we long, and when we are betrayed, how do we keep our own choices?

Four practices on admiration, being needed, desire and relationships | 2026.11

"That's how it is in business. Everyone does it."
"Which successful man doesn't have a few women on the side?"
"Men are all like that. It's in their nature."

You may have heard words like these.

We'd like to say one thing first:

The harm we are talking about here includes deceit, breaking agreements, and ignoring other people's wishes and boundaries. Honestly saying no, or ending a relationship, may also make someone sad, and we still have the right to make those decisions.

Everyone has desires, and anyone can be drawn to someone else. But once you have the feeling, what you do next is still your own choice.

Responsibility for an affair belongs to the person who broke the agreement in the relationship.

No matter how independent and confident you are, or how hard you work on the relationship, nothing can guarantee that your partner will stay faithful.

So if you are the one who got hurt, please remember this first: your partner chose to betray, and the responsibility is not yours. You do not have to answer for someone else's broken promise.

The four practices in this article suit anyone who wants to know themselves better.

Whether you are a man or a woman, and whatever kind of relationship you are going through, we can all try practicing one thing:

Slowly put your sense of worth back in your own hands.

1. Which part of this person do I actually admire?

The strength you admire in someone can also grow in you.

We call this process "disenchantment," which means taking the spell off.

Think about it. Have you ever met someone who seemed especially capable, especially dazzling, and who even seemed to carry a kind of strength you longed for?

It might be their courage, talent, wealth or achievements. It might be their confidence, and how they always seem to know what they want.

Admiring someone is perfectly normal.

But we can ask ourselves a few more questions:

  • What exactly do I admire in them? Their courage, their talent, their wealth, or how they make me feel special?
  • When I see them shine in one area, do I unconsciously assume they can be trusted just as much in other areas?
  • Could the qualities I admire slowly become abilities I have myself, through my own practice?

For example, say you admire someone's courage.
Then try speaking your mind, bravely, the next time you disagree with someone.

Say you admire someone's talent.
Then start learning and creating, and slowly build up work of your own.

Say you admire someone for being wealthy and capable, free to decide how they live.
Then begin by understanding your own finances, managing your income and building your professional skills.

We can treat our admiration for someone as a clue to who we are.

The qualities that draw you in may also show who you long to become.

As you build up these abilities, you may still admire them a lot, and you no longer need to place them so high.

You will slowly realize that success in business does not mean someone is honest in a relationship.

Talent does not mean a person is necessarily responsible.

We can admire someone's strengths and still keep our own judgment.

Even without their achievements, we still have the right to ask questions, refuse requests and hold our boundaries.

We can also like someone without losing ourselves because of it.

Could the strength you admire in them be slowly growing in your own life too?

2. When nobody needs me or idolizes me, how do I see myself?

This question is worth every one of us thinking about.

When we have no money, no achievements, no title, and can't solve anyone's problems for the moment, do we still feel we deserve to be loved?

Some people badly need others to think they are impressive.

Some people enjoy being relied on.

Some people need constant reassurance that they are still attractive and still liked.

These needs can be understood in themselves.

Who doesn't want to be appreciated, affirmed and loved?

But we can try to tell the difference:

We like the person in front of us, and we can also ask ourselves: how much of this liking comes from the feeling of being idolized by them?

When we want to take care of someone, how much of it comes from real concern, and how much from confirming that we matter?

When no one is praising us, no one is looking up to us and no one needs us, can we still look at ourselves with a steady mind?

These questions may be hard to answer all at once.

That's okay. You can think about them slowly.

In some of the environments people grow up in, boys are expected from childhood to be independent, to succeed, to earn money and to be able to take care of others.

Over time, some people may tie their sense of worth to achievement, status and other people's admiration.

Understanding these pressures helps us see the needs behind behavior.

But understanding a cause does not mean we can use it to excuse harm.

If we notice that we badly need to be idolized, relied on or affirmed, we can practice facing that need directly.

Try saying it out loud. Try to understand it.

Try also to build a life where, even if nobody praises us today, we still know who we are.

You can start by writing down: apart from achievements, titles and what I can do for others, which parts of myself do I still value?

Is it being willing to be honest, being able to listen, or being willing to admit a mistake and correct it? All of these can build up slowly through what we do each day.

3. When I feel desire, how do I keep my choice?

Desire, including sexual desire, is something both men and women have. Some men have very low sexual desire, some women have very high sexual desire, and everyone is different. Strong sexual desire does not mean a person will have an affair.

We can first separate three things: sexual desire is a feeling; an impulse is the push to act right away; and behavior is the part you answer for yourself.

Once the body reacts, you still have room to choose what comes next.

You can practice in four steps:

Step one, admit it first: "I feel sexual desire right now."

No need to rush into scolding yourself or feeling dirty, and no need to go looking for someone to satisfy it right away.

First recognize the feeling in your body, and give yourself a little time to decide on the next step.

Step two, ask yourself: "Besides sex, what else do I want right now?"

  • Do I simply want physical pleasure?
  • Do I want to be close to someone, to be touched, to be understood?
  • Do I want to confirm that I'm still attractive?
  • Am I bored or frustrated, and trying to get away from certain feelings for a while?
  • Do I especially care about the feeling of being idolized and needed by the other person?

There can be several answers at once.

It can also be simple: "I just have sexual desire."

You don't have to dig up some trauma or emptiness every time.

Step three, leave some distance between "I want it now" and "I do it now."

For example, stop a flirty conversation, leave a setting where it's easy to cross a line, and put your phone down.

Wait until the impulse eases a little before you decide.

It's best to set boundaries early. If you have already started hiding things or testing the waters, that is the time to stop and take a look.

You can also choose private, safe masturbation, or talk with a willing partner about your needs.

A partner has the freedom to say no, and no one is obliged to take care of another person's sexual desire.

Step four, ask yourself: "How am I willing to take responsibility for this choice?"

At the very least, first check three things:

  • Can everyone involved agree clearly, willingly and without pressure, and change their mind at any time?
  • Does this action fit the relationship agreements my partner and I have already made?
  • Am I hiding anything important that would affect other people's choices?

We can also take apart the phrase "I couldn't help it":

At which point could I actually have stopped?

What should I do earlier next time?

We can understand impulses, and we also answer for what we do.

Some people channel the excitement and energy that desire brings into creating, exercising, or throwing themselves more fully into life.

This is one direction worth trying. It does not promise that desire will go away, and you don't have to turn every bit of desire into productivity.

Desire can be there. You don't have to satisfy it every time.

The thing we most want to leave you with in this section is this question:

"When I want something badly, can I still see myself, the other person and the consequences to come, all at the same time?"

What people call "self-control" really comes down to these four concrete things:

Being able to notice, to pause, to communicate and to keep your agreements.

4. I was betrayed. How do I make decisions again?

This section is for people who are hurting right now.

If you have been betrayed, you may find yourself thinking over and over:

Was I not pretty enough?

Was I not interesting enough?

Was I too busy, too independent, or too dependent on them?

If I had tried a little harder back then, would this have been avoided?

We'd like to remind you:

When someone breaks the agreement in a relationship, that responsibility is theirs to carry. You have no duty to look for your own flaws to explain their betrayal.

You can feel sad, angry or confused.

You can also decide nothing for now.

There is no need to rush to forgive.

There is no need to rush to prove you are strong.

And you certainly don't need to push yourself to become better overnight just to make them regret it.

When your emotions slowly have some room, we can start bringing our attention back to ourselves.

Try asking yourself:

"What facts do I know right now?"

"What support do I need?"

"Where are my boundaries?"

"If we are going to continue, what concrete actions does the other person need to take?"

"Right now, do I want to stay or leave?"

Sometimes we are too used to keeping our attention on the other person.

Why did they do this?

Do they still love me?

Will they change?

But often only the other person can answer these questions, and the answers still have to be confirmed through what they do afterward.

We can also start asking ourselves:

What kind of life do I want to live?

How do I want to be treated in a relationship?

What am I willing to accept, and what has already gone past my boundaries?

Whether a relationship can continue takes the willingness of both people, and it takes concrete action.

You have the right to observe slowly, the right to reconsider, and the right to choose to leave.

If you can't leave right now, there is no need to blame yourself. You can start by taking stock of your finances, housing, caregiving duties and the people who could help, and widen your options one step at a time.

You don't have to keep the relationship going just to prove your own worth.

If this experience is already affecting your eating, sleep or daily life, you can also talk with someone you trust, or seek professional support.

Let yourself have company, and resources to lean on.

As for the life ahead, you can decide it slowly.

In closing

In building Frequency Civilization, we hope to turn these questions into practices anyone can use. You can check whether they match your own experience, and you are welcome to offer a different view.

We can admire a person and still keep our own judgment.

We can long to be loved and also learn to look after our own needs.

We can admit desire and still answer for our own actions.

When others hurt us, we can also, at our own pace, slowly find our way back to the strength to choose how we live.

We hope everyone can gradually build their own sense of worth, without relying on anyone's idolization and without putting anyone on a pedestal.

If you take only one question away from this article, we hope it is this one:

Right now, which choice can I make that respects both my own needs and other people's boundaries?
